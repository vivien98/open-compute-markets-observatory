Every earlier chapter ends in a bilateral, opaque market: wafer allocations, HBM contracts, packaging slots, powered land. This chapter is about the one market at the top of the chain that is almost embarrassingly transparent. For any open-weight model you can open a page and see every provider's price to the cent. The question this chapter answers is why that transparency is not the same thing as truthful prices, and what that tells us about how AI markets will be structured.

The framing follows Tarun Chitra's essay [The Souq: Value Creation and Extraction in OpenRouter](https://robvc.com/research/the-souq), the first of a three-part series from Robot Ventures on open-weight model markets. The empirical findings quoted below are his; the extensions into hardware, Hayek, and decentralized finance are this guide's.

## 9.1 From cathedral to bazaar to souq

Open-source software has a well-understood economics. Eric Raymond's [The Cathedral and the Bazaar](http://www.catb.org/~esr/writings/cathedral-bazaar/) contrasted centrally designed software (the cathedral) with software assembled from many independent contributions (the bazaar). The cloud era rewarded the bazaar, because portable, modular, robust components became more valuable than integrated ones. The economic puzzle of why anyone contributes was worked through by [Lerner and Tirole](https://www.nber.org/papers/w7600): signalling, career concerns, and the fact that the marginal cost of distributing software is zero. The unsolved problem is who pays for maintenance, documented in the Ford Foundation's [Roads and Bridges](https://www.fordfoundation.org/work/learning/research-reports/roads-and-bridges-the-unseen-labor-behind-our-digital-infrastructure/).

The key structural fact is that open source socialized the CapEx of software (developer time, audits) and left the OpEx (servers) to the user. Open-weight models invert this. Training a model is CapEx measured in tens of millions to billions of dollars of compute, borne by the model builder. But the dominant lifetime cost is not the training; it is the electricity and hardware needed to run the model every time someone sends a prompt. In open weights, OpEx dwarfs labor, and OpEx must be paid by somebody on every single request.

That inversion creates a market structure that neither the cathedral nor the bazaar describes. Chitra calls it a souq: a covered market in which independent stalls sell components of one product, and in which the customer rarely deals with the maker of the good directly. In this souq, four kinds of stall have emerged.

## 9.2 The four layers

Start from the user and walk down.

**Harness.** A harness manages a user's data, context, and state, and decides what to send to a model. Coding agents, chat interfaces, and autonomous agents are all harnesses. Their job is to optimize the user's experience: memory, preferences, tool use, skills. They are paid directly by users through subscriptions or usage, and can also be paid by routers for order flow, exactly as retail brokerages are paid for routing stock orders. Chitra's example is Nous Research's [Hermes agent](https://hermes-agent.nousresearch.com/), which at the time of writing accounted for roughly 15-20% of the token flow on OpenRouter.

**Router.** A router maps a task, or a sequence of tasks generated by a harness, to a set of models and to the inference providers that serve them. It charges a fee on routed tokens. [OpenRouter](https://openrouter.ai/) is the canonical example; enterprise platforms such as [Databricks](https://docs.databricks.com/aws/en/machine-learning/model-serving/) and payment companies expanding into AI spend management play the same role. Routing is a B2B business and an increasingly crowded one, because the fee is thin and the capital requirement is low.

**Model builder.** The builder trains and distributes the open-weight model: Z.ai (GLM), Moonshot (Kimi), DeepSeek, MiniMax, Xiaomi, and others. The builder bears the training CapEx and has only two ways to earn it back: operate as an inference provider of its own model, or license. Licensing is a newly live question; Chitra notes that Moonshot's Kimi K3 license requires commercial agreements from operators above a $20 million revenue threshold, an attempt to turn the largest providers into paying customers without closing the weights.

**Inference provider.** The provider owns or rents the compute, loads the weights, and executes the model on the context the harness supplied. Its incentive is simple: maximize tokens received, minimize the cost of executing them. Providers compete on hardware procurement (Chapter 10 traces the GPU-hour cost), on kernel engineering (Together AI's [FlashAttention](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.14135) lineage, Wafer's memory optimizations), and on custom silicon (Cerebras). This is the most capital-intensive and operationally demanding layer, and it is where this chapter connects back to every physical constraint in Chapters 4 and 5.

The harness-to-provider chain is a supply chain in the strict sense of this guide: each layer buys an input from the layer below and sells an output to the layer above. The difference from the silicon chain is that here the layers are decoupled by a standard: almost every open-weight provider speaks the OpenAI-compatible API, so a router can swap providers per request the way a refinery can swap crude cargoes.

## 9.3 Follow one request

To understand the prices, follow one request through the stack, because the prices are defined by what the hardware does at each step.

A harness assembles a prompt: system instructions, tool definitions, conversation history, and the user's new message. This is the input. It goes to the router, which picks a provider and forwards it. The provider runs two very different computations.

**Prefill** processes the entire input in one parallel pass. Every input token attends to every earlier token, producing, for each layer of the network, a key vector and a value vector per token. Those vectors are stored in the KV cache in the accelerator's HBM. Prefill is compute-bound: it is large matrix multiplications, and it is why the input price exists.

**Decode** generates output tokens one at a time. Each new token must read the entire KV cache for the whole context. Decode is therefore memory-bandwidth-bound: the accelerator is limited by how fast HBM can stream the cache, which is why Chapter 4 treats HBM bandwidth as a first-class constraint and why the output price is typically several times the input price.

**Caching** is the observation that most requests in a session share a long prefix (the system prompt, the tool definitions, the conversation so far). If the provider kept the KV cache from the previous request, it does not need to re-run prefill on the shared prefix; it only prefills the new tokens. This is called prefix caching, implemented in engines such as [vLLM](https://docs.vllm.ai/en/latest/features/automatic_prefix_caching.html) and SGLang's [RadixAttention](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.07104), and sold by closed labs as [prompt caching](https://docs.claude.com/en/docs/build-with-claude/prompt-caching). A cached token costs the provider almost nothing, because the only work is a memory lookup; the corresponding price is often a tenth of the input price.

For an engineer, then, the three prices are three physical regimes: a compute-bound regime (input), a bandwidth-bound regime (output), and a lookup regime (cached). For an economist, they are three products with different marginal costs bundled into one API call. The trouble begins because only one of the three is observable to the buyer.

## 9.4 How the router chooses: the Tullock contest

OpenRouter's default routing rule is documented in its [provider routing guide](https://openrouter.ai/docs/guides/routing/provider-selection). Providers that have had an outage in the last 30 seconds are filtered out; among the rest, the router chooses randomly with probability proportional to the inverse square of price. The documentation's own example: if provider A charges $1 per million tokens and provider C charges $3, A is nine times as likely to be picked, because 1/3² = 1/9.

Chitra recognizes this as a [Tullock contest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tullock_contest), the standard model of rent-seeking competitions in which each participant's probability of winning is its effort raised to a decisiveness parameter r, divided by the sum over all participants. Here "effort" is cheapness (1/P), and r = 2. The allocation rule is:

**share of provider i ∝ 1 / Pᵢ²**

With r = 1, halving your price doubles your expected share. With r = 2, halving your price quadruples it. Chitra's phrase is super-linear competition: small relative price changes cause large disparities in expected reward. Measured over 1,648 routing choices, the empirical exponent matched the documented one. Users can override the default with explicit sorts (price, throughput, latency) or the :floor and :nitro [shortcuts](https://openrouter.ai/announcements/introducing-nitro-and-floor-price-shortcuts), but the default is what most token flow experiences.

Two consequences follow immediately. First, a super-linear contest rewards being the cheapest by a lot, so it invites aggressive undercutting rather than gentle competition. Second, the router must rank on some price, and the choice of which price becomes the whole game.

## 9.5 Three prices, one ranking

Each provider quotes three numbers for a model: an input price pᴵ, an output price pᴼ, and a cached price pᶜ, all per million tokens. The router ranks on the input price. Chitra tested 64 cases in which the three prices were not ordered consistently across providers (one provider cheaper on input, another on cached) and found that the router chose the lowest input price every time.

Now consider what a long agentic session actually consists of. A coding agent might send 40 requests in an hour, each carrying a 30,000-token prefix that has not changed and a few hundred tokens that have. If the cache is honoured, the buyer pays pᶜ on 30,000 tokens and pᴵ on a few hundred. If it is not, the buyer pays pᴵ on all of it. Over a session the cached price is where the money moves, and it is the price the router ignores.

This is a textbook case of what Gabaix and Laibson call [shrouded attributes](https://academic.oup.com/qje/article/121/2/505/1884013): the headline price is salient and competed on, the add-on price is obscure and not competed on, and competition on the headline does not discipline the add-on. Printer ink and hotel resort fees are the usual examples. Cached tokens are the AI version, with one aggravation the others lack: the buyer cannot even count how many add-on units were consumed.

## 9.6 Four kinds of provider

Watching a single model market (Z.ai's GLM-5.2, with 32 providers at the last snapshot), Chitra sorts providers into four archetypes. The archetypes matter because each is a different answer to the question "why are you in this contest?"

| Archetype | Behaviour | Examples cited | What they are buying |
| Copy pricers | Quote exactly the model builder's own price; never move | Together AI, Modal, Baseten, Fireworks, Venice, Crusoe, Cloudflare, DigitalOcean | Leaderboard presence and enterprise credibility, not routed share |
| Specialists | Price above the builder, sell speed | Cerebras (wafer-scale hardware), Wafer (kernels) | Latency-sensitive flow that opts out of the price sort |
| Rebaters | Static discount below the builder | Various | A stable share at a stable margin |
| Repricers | Frequent updates, deep undercutting, sometimes 5-10% of the builder's price | StreamLake (a Kuaishou unit), Novita | The super-linear share that the Tullock rule hands to the cheapest |

The copy pricers are the interesting anomaly. Well-funded, venture-backed or public companies quoting a price they know will lose the routing contest are not trying to win it. They are using the router as an advertisement. The repricers are the ones actually competing, and the natural question is how a repricer can quote a tenth of the builder's price and survive.

## 9.7 The caching attack

Part of the answer is that some repricers have cheaper hardware, better kernels, or lower electricity. Chitra's central finding is that another part of the answer is a mechanism that does not require any of those.

The mechanism rests on one engineering fact: **the cached-token count is not verifiable.** The OpenAI-compatible response reports how many tokens were served from cache, and the buyer has no way to check it. A provider can serve a request from a warm cache, do almost no work, and return a response claiming zero cached tokens, thereby charging pᴵ instead of pᶜ for the whole prefix. As Chitra puts it, the components of the API output are not tamper-resistant.

A strategic provider therefore does the following. Cut the quoted input and output prices far below competitors, winning a super-linear share under the Tullock rule. Report no cached tokens regardless of the actual cache state, so every repeated prefix is billed at the input price. Profit on the difference between a cheap-looking headline and a full-price reality. Chitra's stylized example: an honest provider at $1 per million input with $0.01 cached, against a strategic provider at $0.60 input with $0.02 cached; the strategic provider wins the contest and, on a session with a long stable prefix, charges more.

The empirical work used four workloads designed to be cache-friendly and detectable: a canary (deliberately transparent repeated text), a record set (random ledger rows), a conversation (a growing transcript with new questions), and an agent trace (tool-call logs with varying final instructions). Across 159 pricing events the pattern was consistent. After repricing events, repricers, and Novita in particular, recognized 71 percentage points fewer cached tokens than Z.ai serving its own model, and passed on 52 points less of the savings a warm cache should give. Even at half the nominal price, a user on a repricer paid more than a user on the model builder after roughly five repeated queries.

At the market level Chitra estimates the current extraction at about $3.5 million a year, roughly 2% of OpenRouter's revenue of about $140 million; if every provider adopted the strategy, the ceiling would be on the order of $14 million a year, or 10%. The absolute numbers are small. The structural point is not: the most transparent market in the AI stack is one in which the observable price is systematically not the paid price.

## 9.8 Session continuity: why competition can make it worse

There is a second, subtler cost, and it does not require anyone to misbehave. A router that chooses a provider per request has no memory of where the conversation's cache lives. Each turn is handed to whichever provider is a hair cheaper at that instant, and every move lands cold: the new provider has never seen the prefix and must prefill all of it.

Chitra models the expected cost of a session under per-request routing relative to a session-aware router that stays with one provider. With two competitive providers the per-request router costs 2.33 times as much; with 32 providers, 3.63 times. Adding competitors makes it worse, because the probability of landing on the provider that holds your cache falls with the number of providers. This is the AI version of what Klemperer's work on [switching costs](https://academic.oup.com/qje/article-abstract/102/2/375/1922549) describes: the cache is a switching cost that the router's design refuses to recognize, so the market pays it on every switch.

Some routers already behave differently. Databricks commits to a model and provider at the start of a session precisely to preserve cache efficiency. The tradeoff is the one every market designer faces: continuity versus contestability.

## 9.9 Where the value concentrates

Put the pieces together and the shape of the souq becomes visible. Model builders bear the CapEx and are then undercut on their own model by providers who did not pay for it; their only defences are to serve the model themselves or to license, and licensing open weights is a legal experiment. Routers earn a thin fee and face entry from every payments company with an enterprise sales force. Harnesses own the customer, the context, and the switching costs; an agent that remembers your preferences is hard to leave. Inference providers own the hardware and the cache, and, as the caching attack shows, the information asymmetry.

Chitra's conclusion is that value appears to concentrate in two layers: harnesses and inference providers. Readers of Chapter 8 will recognize the pattern. No layer captures value because it is technologically essential. Value goes to whoever holds a scarce, non-substitutable, non-verifiable position, and in the souq those are the customer relationship at the top and the physical execution at the bottom.

## 9.10 Fixing the market

Chitra proposes three levels of remedy, and they map neatly onto the three ways any market handles unverifiable quality.

**Rank on expected cost, not headline.** A router can measure each provider's empirical cache-hit rate hᵢ with its own traffic and rank on the blended price:

**p̄ᵢ = hᵢ · pᶜᵢ + (1 − hᵢ) · pᴵᵢ**

Under this rule, dropping the cache raises the provider's own ranking price, so the attack becomes self-defeating. This is the cheapest fix and requires no new technology.

**Monitor with canaries.** The router runs randomized canary sessions of known cache structure, detects providers whose reported cache counts persistently fall short, and lowers their quality score or the exponent they face until the gap closes. Moonshot's [K2 Vendor Verifier](https://github.com/MoonshotAI/K2-Vendor-Verifier) is a model builder doing the same thing from the other side, checking that providers serve its model faithfully; the [model equality testing](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.20247) literature gives the statistics for detecting a provider that quietly serves a quantized or different model.

**Attest cryptographically.** The strong fix is to require providers to run inside trusted execution environments and produce attestations of their KV-cache counts, for example using [NVIDIA confidential computing](https://www.nvidia.com/en-us/data-center/solutions/confidential-computing/). This makes the cached count verifiable at the hardware level. The cost is performance and exclusion: enclaves slow inference, and older hardware cannot produce them, so mandatory attestation would push out exactly the low-cost providers whose competition the souq depends on.

The three levels are, respectively, a price rule, a surveillance regime, and a hardware standard. Every market with unverifiable quality ends up with some mixture of them.

## 9.11 Analogies that actually hold

Chitra draws four analogies. Each is more than a metaphor; each is a market that solved, or failed to solve, the same verification problem.

**Spoofing in traditional finance.** A spoofer posts orders they intend to cancel, to move the price and trade against the reaction. A repricer posts a price it intends not to honour on the cached leg. Both exploit the fact that the observable order book is not the same as executed intent. Spoofing became a prosecutable offence only once exchanges kept audit trails fine-grained enough to prove intent; the souq has no such audit trail yet.

**Maximal extractable value in blockchains.** [Flash Boys 2.0](https://arxiv.org/abs/1904.05234) showed that whoever orders transactions in a block can extract value from the users whose transactions they order. The router orders requests across providers and, unlike a block builder, does not even see the execution it is ordering. The blockchain response was [proposer-builder separation](https://ethereum.org/en/roadmap/pbs/) and [MEV-Boost](https://docs.flashbots.net/flashbots-mev-boost/introduction): separate the party that chooses the ordering from the party that executes it, and make the choice a sealed-bid auction with a relay that checks validity. A DeFi researcher will notice that the souq already has the separation (router versus provider) but lacks the relay's validity check: nobody verifies that the provider executed what it billed.

**Quality shading in online advertising.** An ad exchange sells impressions of stated quality; a seller can deliver lower quality than promised and the buyer learns only statistically. Ad markets responded with third-party verification, viewability standards, and reputation scores; the canary proposal is the direct analogue.

**The crack spread in oil.** Gasoline trades at a spread to crude, and that spread contains both real refining cost and policy or market manipulation. Tokens trade at a spread to GPU-hours, and that spread contains real inference engineering and, as this chapter shows, some extraction. Chapter 10 develops the crack spread of intelligence in full.

## 9.12 A Hayekian reading

Hayek's claim was that prices economize on knowledge: a buyer who sees the price of tin does not need to know why tin is scarce. The souq is a test of the precondition that makes this work, which Hayek stated but did not emphasize: the price must correspond to the transaction. A quoted price that is not the paid price does not compress knowledge; it destroys it, because it induces every downstream actor to act on a false signal.

Three things follow, and they are the reasons an observatory of AI markets should watch this corner of the stack more closely than its dollar size warrants.

First, the souq is the purest instance of [competition as a discovery procedure](https://cdn.mises.org/qjae5_3_3.pdf) anywhere in the compute chain. Thirty providers with different hardware, kernels, electricity contracts, and cost of capital are discovering what a GLM-class token costs. When it works, the discovered price is the most informative single number about the economics of inference, more informative than any hyperscaler's earnings call, because it is set at the margin by the cheapest honest producer.

Second, the failure is not a failure of competition but of verification, and verification is an engineering property. Whether the market's price signal is true depends on whether the cache count can be checked, which depends on whether the accelerator can attest, which depends on which generation of hardware the provider bought. The knowledge problem and the hardware supply chain are the same problem seen from two sides.

Third, the fixes are themselves market institutions, not central plans. A router that ranks on blended price is an exchange changing its matching rule. A canary is an auditor. An attestation is a warranty. Hayek's spontaneous order does not mean the absence of rules; it means rules that emerge from the participants who have the local knowledge, which in this case are the routers and model builders who can see the anomaly first.

## 9.13 What the observatory watches

The dashboard's token-market panel tracks the quantities this chapter makes legible: the spread between the cheapest quoted input price and the model builder's own price for a reference model; the number of providers per model (the depth of the contest); the implied session cost multiple under per-request routing; and, as a registered gap, the cache-hit rate by provider, which no public source reports and which is the single number that would tell you whether the market's prices are true.

Chapter 10 takes the other direction: from the token price down through the GPU-hour to the megawatt-hour, and out to the futures markets that are beginning to price compute itself.
