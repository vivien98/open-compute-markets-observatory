## 12.1 Deficit, debt, and debt service

A **budget deficit** is a flow: spending minus revenue during a period. **Government debt** is a stock accumulated from past borrowing, valuation changes, and other adjustments. **Debt service** includes interest and, depending on usage, principal repayments; governments normally refinance much principal rather than extinguish the debt.

The US federal government issues Treasury bills, notes, and bonds across maturities. When old debt matures, the Treasury may issue new debt at current rates. Therefore, higher rates feed into total interest cost gradually, not instantly.

## 12.2 Rollover risk

Rollover risk is the danger that maturing debt can be refinanced only at punitive rates or not at all. It is greater with:

- short maturities;
- large near-term financing needs;
- variable-rate debt;
- foreign-currency debt;
- low reserves and weak tax capacity;
- political instability or low creditor confidence.

The [IMF study on debt maturity](https://www.imf.org/en/publications/departmental-papers-policy-papers/issues/2019/02/04/debt-maturity-and-the-use-of-short-term-debt-evidence-form-sovereigns-and-firms-46240) explains why short-term debt exposes sovereigns and firms to renegotiation and crisis risk.

## 12.3 The debt dynamics equation

A useful approximation for the debt-to-GDP ratio d is:

change in d is approximately ((r - g) / (1 + g)) x d - primary balance.

Here r is the effective nominal interest rate, g is nominal GDP growth, and the primary balance is revenue minus non-interest spending as a share of GDP.

Intuition:

If interest exceeds growth and the government runs a primary deficit, debt ratios tend to rise.

If growth exceeds interest, existing debt becomes easier to carry relative to income.

A primary surplus stabilizes debt; a primary deficit adds to it.

For foreign-currency debt, exchange-rate depreciation can suddenly increase the local-currency burden.

## 12.4 Why emerging markets can be hit harder

Many developing countries borrow in dollars or other foreign currencies. A global rate shock can cause investors to move toward safe assets, strengthen the dollar, weaken local currencies, and raise refinancing costs simultaneously. Export earnings and tax revenue may not rise with the debt burden.

The [World Bank International Debt Report 2024](https://openknowledge.worldbank.org/bitstreams/9b3d960c-5f6e-4100-82bc-46d72d4602ad/download) documents the heavy debt-service pressure on developing economies. Country outcomes depend on maturity, currency, reserves, domestic investor base, commodity exposure, IMF access, and policy credibility. Naming a country from debt/GDP alone is not enough.

## 12.5 The Volcker shock and Latin American debt crisis

To reduce US inflation, the Federal Reserve under Paul Volcker sharply tightened monetary policy around 1979-1982. Global nominal rates rose, recession weakened exports, and floating-rate dollar debt became costly. Mexico announced in 1982 that it could not service its debt, and the crisis spread.

The [Federal Reserve History account](https://www.federalreservehistory.org/essays/latin-american-debt-crisis) says sixteen Latin American countries and eleven less-developed countries elsewhere rescheduled debts. This is more precise than the casual claim that roughly forty countries defaulted. The historical analogy is useful, but the current creditor structure, exchange-rate regimes, maturities, reserves, and IMF toolkit differ.

## 12.6 Is the US fiscal arithmetic reasonable?

A commonly cited figure is that debt interest is around 20% of federal revenue. As an order of magnitude, that is plausible. CBO's February 2026 baseline projects $5.6 trillion of revenue and says rising net interest drives much of a $1.9 trillion deficit; its primary deficit excludes interest. See the [CBO 2026 Budget and Economic Outlook](https://www.cbo.gov/publication/61882).

But a "1 percentage point rate increase" does not immediately apply to the entire debt stock. The path depends on:

- which maturities refinance each year;
- the mix of bills, notes, bonds, and inflation-protected securities;
- new deficits;
- inflation and nominal GDP;
- Federal Reserve remittances;
- the yield curve, not one rate.

The US Treasury's [quarterly borrowing presentation](https://home.treasury.gov/system/files/221/TreasuryPresentationToTBACQ22026.pdf) shows the actual maturity and financing framework. A serious stress test models each maturity bucket.

## 12.7 Why the United States is different, but not invulnerable

The United States borrows in a currency it issues, has a deep Treasury market, and supplies a major global reserve asset. It does not face the same foreign-currency constraint as a country with dollar debt. Its limits are inflation, political willingness to tax or cut spending, market confidence, and real productive capacity.

"The US can tax data centers" is only part of the answer. Governments can collect corporate income tax, property tax, sales/use tax, utility tax, payroll tax, and individual income tax from the ecosystem, but incentives, depreciation allowances, profit shifting, ownership structures, and automation affect the base. Economic incidence can fall on owners, workers, customers, landowners, or ratepayers.

## 12.8 Automation and the tax base

Individual income and payroll taxes make up most US federal receipts, while corporate income tax is a smaller share. If automation reduces labor income without replacement, current tax structures collect less. If productivity raises profits, wages, consumption, and asset values, other receipts may grow.

Tax policy can change. A future government could tax profits, consumption, land, electricity, capital income, or compute. This is a real potential mismatch; it should not be read as assuming today's tax code is permanent.

## 12.9 Would AI investment cause a sovereign debt crisis?

The transmission chain is possible:

AI returns and investment demand rise -> corporate borrowing and real-resource demand rise -> benchmark yields and/or spreads rise -> currencies and refinancing conditions tighten -> vulnerable sovereigns face larger interest bills -> adjustment, restructuring, or default.

Every arrow is conditional. The chain weakens if AI CapEx is cash-funded, global saving rises, central banks accommodate without inflation, productivity grows quickly, projects are delayed, or foreign capital expands. It strengthens if investment is debt-heavy, supply is inelastic, inflation rises, and vulnerable countries have short-duration foreign-currency debt.
