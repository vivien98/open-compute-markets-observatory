The [SEC beginner's guide to financial statements](https://www.sec.gov/about/reports-publications/beginners-guide-financial-statements) is the best short foundation for this chapter.

## The three statements

**Balance sheet:** a snapshot of what the company owns and owes. Assets = liabilities + shareholders' equity.

**Income statement:** revenue and expenses over a period, ending in net income.

**Cash-flow statement:** cash from operations, investing, and financing over a period.

One transaction can appear differently across all three. Buying a server reduces cash and adds equipment on the balance sheet; the cash purchase appears in investing cash flow; depreciation expense reaches the income statement gradually.

## Capital expenditure (CapEx)

CapEx is cash spent to acquire or build a long-lived asset. Examples include fab tools, cleanrooms, servers, network equipment, data-center buildings, substations, and turbines.

CapEx is a **cash-flow classification and investment concept**, not normally an immediate income-statement expense. The asset is capitalized, meaning its cost is placed on the balance sheet and allocated over its useful life.

Companies define reported CapEx metrics differently. One may report cash purchases of property and equipment. Another may add principal payments on finance leases. Always read the definition and reconciliation.

## Operating expenditure (OpEx)

OpEx is the recurring cost of running the business during the period. Examples include electricity, ordinary repairs, staff, security, rent, cloud fees, software, property taxes, and consumables. Research and development is usually an operating expense under US GAAP even though it may create future know-how.

The CapEx/OpEx distinction is not "important versus unimportant" or "one-time versus recurring." It depends on whether accounting rules treat the spending as creating or improving a controlled long-lived asset.

## Depreciation

Depreciation spreads the recorded cost of a tangible asset over its estimated useful life. In straight-line depreciation:

annual depreciation = (cost - residual value) / useful life.

Suppose a company buys $100 billion of servers with no assumed residual value and a five-year useful life. Cash falls by $100 billion when it pays, but it recognizes roughly $20 billion of depreciation each year for five years.

This creates two opposite analytical errors:

- treating CapEx as though it were a one-year income-statement expense;
- treating depreciation as "not a real cost" because it is non-cash this year.

Depreciation is non-cash in the current period, but it represents consumption of an asset that required cash and may need replacement.

Actual filings show why estimates matter. Meta's 2025 Form 10-K says most servers and network assets have estimated lives of five to 5.5 years and reports the effect of changing useful lives. Amazon's 2025 Form 10-K says it shortened the estimated life of a subset of servers and network equipment from six to five years due to faster AI/ML technology development. See [Meta's filing](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1326801/000162828026003942/meta-20251231.htm) and [Amazon's filing](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1018724/000101872426000004/amzn-20251231.htm).

## Amortization and impairment

**Amortization** is similar cost allocation for certain intangible assets or finance-lease right-of-use assets. **Impairment** is a write-down when an asset's carrying value is no longer recoverable under the applicable accounting test.

AI infrastructure can face impairment risk if a customer fails, a site cannot be energized, hardware becomes uneconomic, export rules change, or a specialized facility lacks alternative tenants.

## Revenue, gross profit, operating profit, and free cash flow

| Measure | Simplified calculation | What it answers |
| Revenue | Price x quantity | How much customers paid before expenses. |
| Gross profit | Revenue - direct cost of service | Are the core unit economics positive? |
| Operating income | Gross profit - R&D - sales - general OpEx | Does the operating business earn after overhead and research? |
| Net income | Operating income - interest - tax +/- other | Accounting earnings attributable after financing and tax. |
| Operating cash flow | Cash generated by operations after working-capital movements | How much operating cash arrived this period? |
| Free cash flow | Operating cash flow - defined CapEx | What cash remains after reinvestment under the chosen definition? |

When someone says that an early frontier model's inference ran at a negative gross margin, the intended idea is that the direct or allocated cost of serving the model exceeded revenue. When they say newer models generate much more revenue per MW, they are describing unit economics at the serving layer. This does not automatically imply positive net income after R&D, sales, general overhead, interest, taxes, and continued expansion.

## Gross margin versus markup

If compute capacity costs $10 and model revenue is $50:

- gross profit before other direct costs = $40;
- gross margin = $40 / $50 = 80%;
- markup on input cost = $40 / $10 = 400%.

These are not the same percentage. The calculation also depends on what the $10 includes. If it is a cloud rental price, it may include hardware, power, building, financing, and the cloud provider's margin. If it is only hardware depreciation, the cost base is incomplete.

## Revenue per MW and the hidden time denominator

Take a commonly quoted pair of figures and assume they are annualized:

- capacity cost: $15 million per MW-year;
- model revenue: $50 million per MW-year.

Then 1 GW produces $50 billion of annual revenue and incurs $15 billion of capacity cost, leaving $35 billion before other costs. The implied gross margin on this simplified basis is 70%.

If the period is a multiyear contract value rather than one year, the result changes completely. A professional model would specify:

- contract term and ramp schedule;
- capacity availability and utilization;
- power boundary and PUE;
- chip generation and performance;
- token price and product mix;
- revenue share or cloud credits;
- electricity, network, support, and model-development allocations.

## EBITDA and why it can flatter a capital-heavy business

EBITDA is earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation, and amortization. It can help compare operations before financing and non-cash cost allocation, but it can overstate economic cash generation when equipment must be replaced frequently. An AI infrastructure business can show attractive EBITDA while consuming enormous cash through CapEx.

## Free cash flow

A common simplified formula is:

free cash flow = cash from operations - capital expenditures.

Definitions vary. Meta, for example, defines its non-GAAP free cash flow by subtracting purchases of property and equipment and principal payments on finance leases from operating cash flow in its [2025 Form 10-K](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1326801/000162828026003942/meta-20251231.htm).

Free cash flow can be negative during a valuable build because cash is invested before the asset earns revenue. It can also be negative because the investment is bad. The number alone does not decide.

## Return on invested capital

Return on invested capital compares after-tax operating profit with the capital required to produce it. It is more informative than a fab-CapEx-to-end-revenue ratio because it tries to match profit with all capital employed.

For any project, ask:

What is the total installed cost?

When does the asset become operational?

What utilization and price will it achieve?

What are operating costs and taxes?

How fast does the asset depreciate economically?

What working capital is required?

What is the residual value?

What discount rate matches the risk?
