## Internal cash flow

A profitable hyperscaler can use cash generated by advertising, cloud services, commerce, or software. This is cheap and flexible because there is no new lender or shareholder claim. But it has an opportunity cost: the cash could fund buybacks, dividends, acquisitions, or other projects.

Stopping a buyback does not "create" economic resources. It redirects the company's claim on available saving and real inputs from shareholders toward infrastructure.

## Equity

A company can sell ownership stakes. Equity absorbs losses before debt and has no fixed repayment date, but it dilutes existing owners and investors demand a high expected return for bearing risk.

Venture funding historically financed lab losses before the labs had reliable operating cash flow. As unit economics improve, new equity can finance faster expansion rather than survival.

## Corporate bonds and loans

A bond is a tradable promise to pay interest and principal. A bank loan is negotiated with one or more lenders. The borrowing rate can be decomposed roughly as:

borrower's yield = risk-free benchmark + term premium + credit spread + liquidity/structure adjustments.

The **risk-free benchmark** is often a Treasury yield or swap rate of similar maturity.

The **term premium** compensates for interest-rate and duration risk.

The **credit spread** compensates for default and downgrade risk, uncertainty, and liquidity.

Covenants, collateral, seniority, and guarantees affect the price.

A basis point is 0.01 percentage point. A move from 5.5% to 8.0% is 250 basis points.

If a company borrows $100 billion:

- at 6%, annual coupon interest is about $6 billion;
- at 8.5%, it is about $8.5 billion;
- the extra 250 bps costs $2.5 billion per year before tax effects.

## Leases

A lease separates asset ownership from use. A data-center developer may own the building while a hyperscaler or lab commits to payments. Accounting distinguishes operating and finance leases, but both create real fixed obligations.

Meta's 2025 filing reports large leases not yet commenced, mostly for data centers, colocation, and network infrastructure, with commencement dates in later years. This is exactly the lead-lag pattern: a contract is signed before the asset is available.

## Capacity contracts, take-or-pay, and reservations

A cloud or developer may require a customer to reserve a minimum amount of capacity and pay even if it uses less. This is called take-or-pay or committed consumption. The contract reduces demand risk for the builder.

The key credit insight is:

customer commitment -> predictable project cash flow -> lender confidence -> construction capital.

A liquid compute futures market ({ch:token-prices}) could eventually substitute for part of this: a builder that sells its rental revenue forward on an exchange gives the lender a cleared hedge instead of a bilateral commitment, which is how merchant power plants came to be financed. The contract does not remove risk. Lenders examine the customer's credit quality, termination rights, performance conditions, construction risk, technology obsolescence, power availability, and whether the asset can be re-leased.

## Project finance and special-purpose vehicles

A developer may place one campus in a special-purpose vehicle (SPV). Equity sponsors contribute first-loss capital. Banks or bondholders lend against project assets and contracted cash flows. The debt may be **non-recourse** or **limited-recourse**, meaning lenders primarily rely on the project rather than the sponsor's entire balance sheet.

A simplified life cycle is:

Secure land, interconnection, permits, and a customer commitment.

Raise sponsor equity and construction debt.

Order long-lead equipment and build.

Complete performance tests and begin the lease.

Refinance expensive construction debt with longer-term debt once cash flow is stable.

Pay operating costs, interest, principal, and then equity distributions through a contractual waterfall.

## Asset-backed and infrastructure funds

Pension funds, insurers, private infrastructure funds, banks, and private credit can own or lend against data centers, fiber, power assets, or equipment. Their liabilities and return requirements differ. An insurer may like long, predictable contracted cash flows; a private-credit fund may accept construction or customer concentration risk for a higher yield.

Calling all of this "hyperscaler debt" misses the migration of obligations through landlords, utilities, suppliers, SPVs, and private funds. The primary documents are the filings themselves: [EDGAR full-text search](https://efts.sec.gov/LATEST/search-index?q=%22take-or-pay%22&dateRange=custom) for the phrase "take-or-pay" surfaces the commitments notes where these obligations appear, and a neocloud's 10-K risk-factor section is the most candid public description of customer concentration in the industry.

## Vendor financing, prepayments, and strategic investment

Suppliers can support customers through payment terms, equipment financing, or investment. Customers can prepay to secure supply. Chip firms can invest in clouds or labs to stimulate demand. Governments can provide grants, tax credits, loans, or infrastructure.

These arrangements change who bears risk and when cash moves. They do not make the physical build free.

## Prepayments and the small buyer

Everything above describes how a large, creditworthy customer makes a project financeable. A customer without an investment-grade rating has to substitute cash for credit. The standard device is a prepayment of 20% to 50% of total contract value, paid at signing, weeks before the cluster exists. It protects the operator against the customer's default and, because it arrives before the hardware is paid for, it also funds the operator's own equity in the project. {ch:collateral} works through the arithmetic and shows why lenders insist on it: there is no resale price for the hardware that a lender could rely on instead. {ch:commodity} follows the consequence, which is that the length of contract a buyer can obtain depends on its credit and not on its need.

## Why "most compute is contracted before it is built"

A lender does not want to fund a specialized $10 billion asset with no tenant. A signed commitment makes future revenue legible. The tradeoff is bargaining power: a developer that builds speculatively with a strong balance sheet may later sell scarce ready-to-use capacity at a premium, but it bears vacancy, construction, and market risk until then.

This is the **optionality** held by a balance-sheet-rich builder that can afford to build ahead of contracts. Owning uncommitted capacity lets the owner choose between internal use and external rental after learning more about prices and model quality.

| Line | Illustrative annual or one-time figure | Boundary / risk |
| Logic-fab enabling CapEx | $6B one time | Thought experiment ({ch:silicon}); only a manufacturing increment. |
| Critical IT purchase | $30B-$50B one time | Accelerators, servers, networking; generation-dependent. |
| Building and power | $5B-$15B one time | Site, electrical and cooling boundary varies widely. |
| Compute capacity rent | $15B per GW-year | Equals $15M per MW-year; assumes full contracted capacity. |
| Lab revenue | $50B per GW-year | Equals $50M per MW-year; highly sensitive to product and utilization. |
| Simplified spread | $35B per year | Before model R&D, staff, tax, other direct costs, downtime and financing. |
| Debt | $25B at 7% | $1.75B annual interest before amortization and fees. |

This example is intentionally stylized. It shows why a contract price per MW cannot be compared directly with a fab's tool CapEx. Each figure lives at a different boundary and time.

## Debt capacity and coverage ratios

Lenders often examine:

**Interest coverage:** operating earnings or cash flow divided by interest.

**Debt service coverage ratio (DSCR):** cash available for debt service divided by required principal plus interest.

**Loan-to-value:** debt divided by asset value.

**Leverage:** debt relative to EBITDA, equity, or cash flow.

**Minimum liquidity:** cash or committed facilities available during stress.

A project with $3 billion of annual cash available for debt service and $2 billion of scheduled debt service has DSCR 1.5x. If price or utilization falls by one-third, coverage may disappear.

## The maturity wall

Debt has a maturity date. Refinancing risk arises when a borrower must replace maturing debt at unknown future rates. Short maturity can be cheap today but exposes the borrower sooner. Long maturity locks financing but may cost more.

This same concept drives the sovereign-debt chapter ({ch:sovereign-debt}).
